Detailed explantions on data results


Project overview

Comparison of sea ice and no sea ice per cell among observed regions from GMASI, IceLynx and VIIRS.


Projection used for plotting and calculations

The sea sectors and sea ice have been converted to PROJ+4 projection. This projection resulted in the most accurate distance and bearing calculations as well as positional plotting. The projection closely resembles EPSG:3413, but with different longitudinal alignment. The parameters `+x_0=0 +y_0=0 +datum=WGS84 +units=m +no_defs +type=crs` can be found at https://nsidc.org/data/user-resources/help-center/guide-nsidcs-polar-stereographic-projection.


Resampling overview

GMASI and IceLynx operate at different spatial resolutions. GMASI is a 2 km global product on a geographic (EPSG:4326) grid, while IceLynx is a 1 km product on a polar stereographic (EPSG:3413) grid. Direct pixel-to-pixel comparison is therefore not possible without first aligning the two grids. Three strategies are evaluated: upsampling (increasing GMASI resolution to match IceLynx), downsampling (reducing IceLynx resolution to match GMASI), and regridding (projecting GMASI onto the IceLynx 1 km polar stereographic grid). Within each strategy, multiple resampling algorithms are tested to assess how the choice of algorithm affects agreement between the two products.


Upsampling (GMASI to IceLynx resolution)

Upsampling increases the spatial resolution of GMASI from its native 2 km grid to match the 1 km IceLynx grid. Each 2 km GMASI pixel is subdivided and its value is distributed across the finer IceLynx grid cells using the chosen resampling algorithm. Metrics are then computed on the IceLynx grid, treating IceLynx as the reference dataset. Upsampling preserves the full spatial detail of IceLynx while introducing interpolated values from GMASI.


Downsampling (IceLynx to GMASI resolution)

Downsampling reduces the spatial resolution of IceLynx from its native 1 km grid to match the 2 km GMASI grid. Multiple 1 km IceLynx pixels are aggregated into each 2 km GMASI cell using the chosen resampling algorithm. Metrics are computed on the GMASI grid, treating GMASI as the reference dataset. Downsampling reduces the influence of fine-scale spatial variability in IceLynx and tests whether agreement improves when both products are evaluated at the coarser resolution.


Regridding (GMASI to IceLynx 1 km polar stereographic grid)

Regridding reprojects GMASI from its native geographic coordinate system (EPSG:4326) onto the IceLynx 1 km polar stereographic grid (EPSG:3413). Unlike simple upsampling, regridding also accounts for the change in map projection, correcting for the distortion introduced when comparing a global geographic grid with a polar-optimised grid. This approach is the most geometrically rigorous of the three strategies and is particularly relevant for Arctic regions where geographic grids introduce significant area distortion at high latitudes.


Rio resampler (rasterio)

The rio resampler uses the rasterio library to reproject raster data between grids. Multiple algorithm variants are evaluated: bilinear interpolation computes the output value as a weighted average of the four nearest source pixels; bicubic interpolation uses a 4x4 neighbourhood for a smoother result; nearest-neighbour assigns the value of the single closest source pixel without interpolation; average computes the mean of all source pixels that contribute to each target cell; and mode assigns the most frequently occurring value among contributing source pixels. Bilinear and bicubic are continuous interpolators suited to fractional ice concentration, while nearest and mode are categorical interpolators that preserve discrete ice or water labels.


KD-tree resampler (pyresample)

The KD-tree resampler uses a k-dimensional tree spatial index to find the nearest source pixels for each target grid cell, implemented via the pyresample library. A Gaussian distance-weighting function is applied so that closer source pixels contribute more to the output value. The key parameters are region of influence (ROI), which defines the search radius in metres around each target cell; sigma, which controls the width of the Gaussian weighting kernel; and the maximum number of neighbours to consider within the ROI. Larger ROI and sigma values produce smoother results by incorporating more distant source pixels, while smaller values produce sharper results closer to nearest-neighbour behaviour. The KD-tree approach is well suited to irregular or swath-based source grids and handles the polar stereographic to geographic reprojection implicitly through coordinate-based proximity search.


Bucket resampler

The bucket resampler aggregates all source pixels whose centres fall within each target grid cell, computing summary statistics over that collection. Because each target cell acts as a spatial bucket collecting nearby source values, this approach naturally handles cases where source and target pixels differ substantially in size. The mean ice fraction is computed for each bucket, then thresholded to produce a binary ice or water classification. The bucket resampler is robust to irregular source sampling density and avoids the interpolation artefacts that can arise with kernel-based methods near coastlines or ice edges.


Ice concentration threshold

Several resampling algorithms produce a continuous ice fraction between 0 and 1 rather than a binary ice or water label. A threshold is applied to convert this fraction to a binary classification: pixels with an ice fraction at or above the threshold are classified as ice (1), and pixels below the threshold are classified as open water (0). The choice of threshold affects the balance between false positives and false negatives. A low threshold (e.g. 0.1) classifies nearly all cells with any ice presence as ice, increasing recall but potentially reducing precision. A high threshold (e.g. 0.7) requires a strong ice signal before classifying a cell as ice, increasing precision but potentially missing marginal ice. Thresholds of 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7 are evaluated to characterise this sensitivity. Nearest-neighbour and mode resamplers produce binary outputs directly and do not use a threshold.


Accuracy

Accuracy measures the overall fraction of correctly classified pixels, considering both ice and open water. It is defined as the ratio of true positives and true negatives to all evaluated pixels: \[ \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}. \] Here, $TP$ is the number of true positives (both datasets report ice), $TN$ is the number of true negatives (both report open water), $FP$ is the number of false positives (dataset 1 reports ice while dataset 2 reports open water), and $FN$ is the number of false negatives (dataset 1 reports open water while dataset 2 reports ice).


Precision (Positive Predictive Value)

Precision quantifies how reliable the ice detections are in dataset 1. It answers: of all pixels labeled as ice by dataset 1, what fraction are also ice in dataset 2? \[ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}. \] High precision means there are few false-ice detections (low $FP$). When $TP + FP = 0$, precision is undefined and reported as NaN.


Sensitivity (True Positive Rate)

Recall, also known as sensitivity or true positive rate, measures how well dataset 1 recovers the ice pixels identified by dataset 2. It answers: of all true ice pixels in dataset 2, what fraction are correctly labeled as ice by dataset 1? \[ \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}. \] High recall means few ice pixels are missed (low $FN$). When $TP + FN = 0$, recall is undefined and reported as NaN.


Specificity (True Negative Rate)

Specificity, or true negative rate, measures how well dataset 1 correctly identifies open water. It answers: of all open-water pixels in dataset 2, what fraction are also labeled as open water by dataset 1? \[ \text{Specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}. \] High specificity means few water pixels are incorrectly flagged as ice (low $FP$). When $TN + FP = 0$, specificity is undefined and reported as NaN.


F1 denominator (precision + recall)

The intermediate variable \texttt{f1\_den} is simply the sum of precision and recall: \[ \text{f1\_den} = \text{Precision} + \text{Recall}. \] It appears in the denominator of the F1 score. When this sum is zero or not finite, the F1 score cannot be computed and is reported as NaN.


F1 Score

The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a single measure that balances both false positives and false negatives: \[ F_1 = \frac{2\,\text{Precision}\,\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}. \] The F1 score is high only when both precision and recall are high. It is especially useful when the classes (ice vs open water) are imbalanced. When $\text{Precision} + \text{Recall} = 0$, the F1 score is undefined and reported as NaN.


Balanced Accuracy

Balanced accuracy averages the performance on the positive class (ice) and the negative class (open water) by combining recall (sensitivity) and specificity. It is defined as \[ \text{Balanced Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Recall} + \text{Specificity}}{2}. \] This metric is particularly useful when the number of ice and open-water pixels is highly imbalanced, preventing an over-optimistic assessment that can occur when one class dominates.
